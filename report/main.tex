\section*{ABSTRACT}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

Digitizing historical documents is often difficult due to significant character and word errors introduced during the Optical Character Recognition (OCR) process. While modern AI models offer powerful correction capabilities, they often struggle to distinguish between actual OCR noise and valid historical orthography, leading to the risk of "over-correction."

This study evaluates two different architectural paradigms to solve this problem: a standard encoder-decoder model (BART) and a modern Large Language Model (Qwen) trained efficiently using QLoRA. Using the multilingual HIPE-OCRepair 2026 dataset, we analyze the trade-off between error reduction and historical fidelity.

Our results show that while Large Language Models (LLMs) achieve better semantic understanding, they are more prone to aggressive paraphrasing and hallucinations. In contrast, standard autoencoder models (Seq2Seq) maintain tighter lexical constraints at the cost of contextual fluidity. Finally, we propose a qualitative error taxonomy to categorize these behaviors, helping researchers understand how to safely use generative AI to clean up historical archives without losing their original character.

\textbf{Keywords}: OCR Post-Correction, Historical Documents, Large Language Models (LLMs), Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq), Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT), Over-correction

\subsection{Context and Problem Statement}
As the world moves toward digital records, historical documents are also being digitized. These archives are important sources for researchers to understand past events, social changes, and cultural perspectives. While scanning and Optical Character Recognition (OCR) technology improve the accessibility of these documents, the extracted text is often noisy and full of errors. The quality of OCR frequently suffers due to physical degradation over time (such as bleed-through, ink spills, and fading), poor print quality, outdated typefaces, and complex page layouts. Consequently, this noise significantly hinders text mining techniques and keyword searches, making it difficult for scholars to extract meaningful information. To unlock the full potential of these primary sources, post-OCR correction techniques, such as refining and enhancing the generated text, are crucial. 

However, working with historical text involves more than just reading degraded scans. Historical documents frequently contain archaic spellings, unique grammar, and vocabulary that are no longer used today. In recent years, Large Language Models (LLMs) have significantly advanced the field of natural language processing, demonstrating remarkable success in analyzing complex context and generating coherent text with minimal training \cite{liang2023holisticevaluationlanguagemodels, zhao2026surveylargelanguagemodels}. Yet, applying these state-of-the-art models to historical OCR post-correction introduces significant challenges. Standard language models are primarily trained on modern text. Without specialized training, they tend to "over-correct" the text, replacing valid historical words with modern equivalents. Furthermore, distinguishing between actual OCR noise and a valid historical spelling variation is a highly complex task. When faced with degraded text, models can struggle to guess the correct word, sometimes generating text that alters the original meaning. Therefore, there is a critical need to evaluate how well different model architectures can reduce scanning errors while keeping the historical authenticity of the document intact.

\subsection{Proposed Solution}

To address the dual challenge of reducing OCR noise and preventing over-correction, this thesis evaluates several modern deep learning models. Instead of relying on a single architecture, this study compares different approaches across four main experiments:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq) Models:} We evaluate the standard encoder-decoder approach by fine-tuning models from the BART family \cite{lewis2019bartdenoisingsequencetosequencepretraining}. This includes testing different model sizes (Base vs. Large) and exploring multilingual models (mBART).
    
    \item \textbf{Decoder-Only (Generative) Models:} We investigate modern Large Language Models (LLMs), such as Qwen3 \cite{yang2025qwen3technicalreport}. We compare different ways to use them, from simple Zero-Shot prompting to full Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).
    
    \item \textbf{Domain-Specific Models:} We compare our general models against systems that were specifically built and pre-trained for OCR correction. This includes specialized Seq2Seq models (like PyKale \cite{thomas-etal-2024-leveraging}) and specialized generative models (like PleIAs \cite{pleIAs}).
    
    \item \textbf{The Impact of Metadata:} We conduct specific experiments to see if adding extra context (metadata) to the prompts during fine-tuning improves a generative model's ability to correct historical text.
\end{itemize}

By comparing these different methods, this study explores the current state of OCR post-correction. We will evaluate each approach quantitatively by comparing different metrics before and after correction. Furthermore, we will provide a detailed qualitative analysis, using specific examples to explain why these models succeed or fail in enhancing historical documents. The detailed results and architectural comparisons will be discussed in the following chapters.

%================================= BACKGROUND & RELATED WORK ======================================
\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{OCR in Historical Documents}
Optical Character Recognition (OCR) is a method of extracting textual content from images of documents. This procedure is frequently required when a digital copy of the text is unavailable, such as when processing physical archives. For decades, massive volumes of scanned documents have been processed using traditional OCR algorithms, driving major digitization efforts by Google Books \cite{leetaru_2008}, the Europeana Newspapers Project \cite{neudecker_2016}, and the National Library of Australia \cite{holley_2009}. 

While modern state-of-the-art models based on deep learning have significantly improved baseline accuracy, substantial noise often persists in the output. This residual noise severely degrades the performance of downstream natural language processing tasks, such as named entity recognition, part-of-speech tagging, and text summarization \cite{10.1007/978-3-030-64452-9_3,vanStrien2020AssessingTI}. 

These limitations are particularly pronounced when OCR is applied to historical documents. Unlike modern text, historical archives present a unique set of interconnected challenges that heavily disrupt OCR engines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical Degradation:} Centuries-old documents frequently suffer from severe physical aging, including faded ink, paper discoloration, and ink bleed-through from the opposite side of the page.
    \item \textbf{Archaic Typography:} Historical texts often feature non-standard or obsolete fonts (such as Fraktur in German documents), as well as deteriorated lead types and inconsistent spacing.
    \item \textbf{Layout Complexity:} The structure of historical newspapers and books often includes dense, multi-column layouts, marginalia, and inconsistent reading orders that confuse standard OCR bounding boxes.
\end{itemize}

Because of these physical and structural ambiguities, the resulting OCR output is often so degraded that even human interpretation becomes subjective. Consequently, robust post-correction pipelines are required to reconstruct the text before it can be effectively analyzed by scholars.

\subsection{OCR Post-Correction as a Task}
To mitigate the limitations of raw OCR engines, early post-correction techniques primarily relied on rule-based methods and dictionary lookups to fix out-of-vocabulary words. However, these traditional systems struggle significantly with historical documents. Because historical texts are full of archaic spellings, static dictionaries often flag valid historical words as errors, unintentionally replacing them with modern equivalents.

To overcome this, the field shifted toward data-driven machine learning, fundamentally reformulating OCR correction as a "translation" problem. In this modern paradigm, a neural network is trained to map a sequence of noisy input text directly to a sequence of clean output text. By treating correction as a sequence-to-sequence translation, models can move beyond isolated word lookups and learn the complex, context-dependent error patterns caused by historical document degradation.

\subsection{Neural Approaches for Text Correction}
To address this translation task, modern research relies on deep learning architectures. This study focuses on comparing the two dominant paradigms in the field.

\subsubsection{Sequence-to-Sequence Models (BART)}
Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq) architectures, such as BART (Lewis et al. \cite{lewis2019bartdenoisingsequencetosequencepretraining}), have become a standard baseline for text correction. Utilizing an encoder-decoder structure, these models are designed to read the entire noisy input before generating the clean output. This structural alignment makes them highly effective at resolving local, character-level distortions (like faded letters or arbitrary spaces) while strictly adhering to the original length and structure of the text.

\subsubsection{Large Language Models (LLMs) and Hallucinations}
More recently, autoregressive Large Language Models (LLMs) with decoder-only architectures, such as Qwen3 (introduced in 2025 by Alibaba \cite{yang2025qwen3technicalreport}), have been applied to post-correction. These models possess a massive capacity to understand broad semantic context, allowing them to guess missing words in highly degraded text. 

However, because LLMs are primarily pre-trained on modern internet corpora, their generative nature introduces major risks for historical documents. They frequently suffer from "over-correction," where the model automatically modernizes valid historical spellings to fit up-to-date grammar rules. Furthermore, they are prone to "hallucinations"—inventing 
reasonable but historically inaccurate content that was never in the original scan. This creates a fundamental trade-off between achieving semantic fluency and preserving archival authenticity.

\subsubsection{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)}
To prevent LLMs from over-correcting, they must be specialized (fine-tuned) on historical text. Traditionally, fine-tuning an LLM requires massive computational resources that are unavailable to standard research laboratories. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) techniques, specifically QLoRA (Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation)\cite{dettmers2023qloraefficientfinetuningquantized}, solve this hardware bottleneck. By freezing the original model weights and training only a small, highly compressed set of new parameters, QLoRA allows massive models to adapt to the specific nuances of historical orthography using a single standard GPU.

\section{Methodology and Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Task Definition}
The primary objective of this study is to perform OCR post-correction on degraded historical documents. Formally, this is defined as a sequence mapping task where the input sequence $X$ is a noisy text segment generated by an OCR engine, and the output sequence $Y$ is the curated, clean text. 

However, unlike standard spell-checking tasks, this research operates under a strict linguistic constraint: \textbf{the model must reduce scanning noise while preserving historical orthography.} The core challenge is training the models to accurately distinguish between a genuine OCR degradation (e.g., correcting the scanning error "t\textbf{lie}" to "t\textbf{he}") and a valid historical spelling variation (e.g., preserving the archaic French "précéd\textbf{è}nt" instead of modernizing it to "précéd\textbf{e}nt").


\subsection{The HIPE-OCRepair Benchmark}
To train and evaluate the models, this study utilizes the HIPE-OCRepair benchmark \cite{hipe_ocrepair_2026}. This dataset is specifically designed for the ICDAR 2026 competition and provides a standardized environment for OCR post-correction on historical documents. It covers three languages: English, French, and German, originating from established archives such as DTA-19, Impresso, Overproof, and subsets of previous ICDAR 2017 competitions.

\subsubsection{Data Format and Structure}
A key characteristic of this dataset is that the models do not have access to the original scanned images; they must perform the correction purely based on text and context. The data is provided in JSONL format, where each text segment contains the \textbf{OCR Hypothesis} (the noisy input), the \textbf{Curated Ground Truth} (the clean target), and \textbf{Document Metadata} (language, publication date, and origin).

\subsubsection{Data Preprocessing and Chunking Strategy}
A major technical challenge in OCR post-correction is sequence length. Historical documents frequently exceed the strict 1024-token context window limitations of transformer models like BART. To address this without losing contextual integrity, we developed a custom preprocessing pipeline to dynamically partition the documents.

Instead of employing naive, rigid truncation, which risks cutting off words mid-sentence and destroying semantic context, the pipeline utilizes a sequence-matching algorithm to identify exact textual anchor points between the noisy OCR sequence and the ground truth. The algorithm partitions the text into manageable chunks (targeting approximately 300 words) while explicitly prioritizing punctuation boundaries (e.g., periods, exclamation marks) within a flexible search window. 

This guarantees that both the input text and the target text remain perfectly aligned and grammatically whole. During the inference phase, these aligned chunks are processed independently by the models and subsequently stitched back together to reconstruct the full document for evaluation. Finally, the partitioned data was isolated into distinct sub-datasets based on language (English, French, German) and aggregated into a combined multilingual dataset to facilitate targeted training.

\subsection{Experimental Models}
To evaluate how different architectures handle the trade-off between error reduction and historical preservation, we compare two distinct neural paradigms: a traditional Sequence-to-Sequence baseline and a modern Generative Large Language Model.

\subsubsection{The Seq2Seq Baseline: BART Fine-Tuning}
We employ the BART (Bidirectional and Auto-Regressive Transformers) family of models as our baseline. Because BART utilizes a full encoder-decoder architecture, it is naturally suited for text restoration tasks. The encoder processes the entire noisy OCR sequence to build a bidirectional context, while the decoder autoregressively generates the corrected text. 

In this experiment, BART is fine-tuned strictly as a translation model. It receives no explicit instructions or metadata; it simply learns the statistical mapping from the noisy input domain to the clean target domain. This architecture generally enforces strict adherence to the input length and structure, making it a highly constrained baseline.

\subsubsection{The LLM Challenger: Qwen Fine-Tuning with QLoRA}
To represent the modern generative approach, we utilize Qwen, an autoregressive decoder-only Large Language Model. Unlike BART, Qwen is instruction-tuned. The model is guided by a system prompt that explicitly defines its persona and task (e.g., \textit{"You are an expert archivist correcting OCR errors in historical documents..."}), followed by the noisy text.

Because fully fine-tuning an LLM with billions of parameters is computationally prohibitive and prone to catastrophic forgetting, we adapt Qwen using Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT). Specifically, we implement Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA). In this setup, the base weights of the Qwen model are frozen and quantized to 4-bit precision to fit within standard GPU memory limits. We then introduce and train small, low-rank matrices (LoRA adapters) specifically on the HIPE-OCRepair dataset. This allows the LLM to learn the specific orthographic rules of historical texts without losing its massive pre-trained semantic knowledge.

\subsection{Model Training and Setup}
To guarantee reproducibility and an equitable comparison, both models were trained under controlled hardware conditions on L3i’s calculmaster SLURM nodes.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
Evaluating OCR post-correction models on historical documents requires a precise assessment of how much noise is successfully removed without introducing new generative errors. This study employs standard edit-distance metrics alongside relative improvement scores to quantitatively measure model performance.

\subsubsection{Quantitative Metrics (CER \& WER)}
To measure the absolute volume of OCR noise, we utilize two standard string-matching metrics based on the Levenshtein distance introduced in 1965. In our evaluation pipeline, these metrics are computed using the \texttt{jiwer} Python library:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Character Error Rate (CER):} Measures the minimum number of character-level edit operations required to transform the model's output into the curated ground truth. It is formally calculated as:
    \begin{equation}
        CER = \frac{S + D + I}{N}
    \end{equation}
    where $S$ is the number of substitutions, $D$ is the number of deletions, $I$ is the number of insertions, and $N$ is the total number of characters in the ground truth reference. It serves as the primary metric for capturing subtle orthographic improvements.
    
    \item \textbf{Word Error Rate (WER):} Applies the exact same distance calculation at the token (word) level. The formula remains identical, but $S$, $D$, $I$, and $N$ represent words rather than characters. WER serves as a secondary metric to evaluate overall readability and lexical reconstruction.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Relative Error Reduction}
While absolute CER and WER provide a snapshot of the final text quality, they do not immediately illustrate the actual value added by the post-correction model relative to the original degraded input. To measure the true efficiency of the models, we calculate the Relative Error Reduction for both characters ($\Delta CER$) and words ($\Delta WER$).

The relative improvement is defined as the percentage reduction in errors from the baseline OCR text to the model's corrected output. For CER, the formula is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta CER = \frac{CER_{ocr} - CER_{model}}{CER_{ocr}} \times 100
\end{equation}

The exact same mathematical formulation is applied to compute $\Delta WER$. 

A positive percentage indicates a successful reduction in OCR noise, whereas a negative percentage would indicate that the model introduced more errors than it fixed (e.g., through severe hallucinations or aggressive over-correction). By tracking both the absolute scores and the relative reduction percentages, we can systematically compare the operational efficiency of the BART and Qwen architectures.

\subsubsection{Training and Evaluation Strategies}
To thoroughly assess the models' capacity for both domain adaptation and cross-lingual generalization, we implement two distinct experimental workflows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dataset-Level Protocol (Domain-Specific):} In this localized setup, a model is fine-tuned and evaluated strictly within the boundaries of a single historical collection. For instance, the model is trained exclusively on the \texttt{dta19\_train} split and subsequently evaluated on the \texttt{dta19\_test} split. This approach establishes a baseline for how well the architecture masters a specific historical period, language, and layout.
    
    \item \textbf{Aggregation-Level Protocol (Cross-Domain):} To evaluate generalized learning, all available training datasets are merged into a single, unified multilingual corpus (\texttt{hipe\_aggregated\_train}). The model fine-tuned on this comprehensive dataset is then independently evaluated against each individual test set (e.g., \texttt{dta19\_test}, \texttt{icdar2017\_test}, \texttt{french\_test}). This protocol reveals whether exposure to a highly diverse dataset creates beneficial transfer learning across languages, or if it causes interference that degrades domain-specific accuracy.
\end{itemize}
